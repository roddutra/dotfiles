# Feature requests

Open this when they ask for a feature, or describe their ideal tool. The request is true as a wish and tells you nothing yet. The same words can mean a small convenience, a cost paid every week, or the reason a tool was once rejected. Work backwards from it, one question at a time, with `MOVE: dig`:

1. **The outcome.** What having it would let them get done. *"What would that let you finish that you can't now?"*
2. **The last time it mattered.** A real occasion when they went without it. *"When did you last need that and not have it?"*
3. **The workaround.** How the job got done anyway, and whether that is good enough.
4. **The consequence.** What the gap cost on that occasion.
5. **The constraint.** Whether it ever decided anything: a tool chosen, refused or dropped because of it, and who made that call.

Then test it: on that occasion, was there another route to the same outcome, and did they try it? Ask about the attempt, not about what they would prefer.

Do not discuss the idea, improve on it, or say whether it could be built. Keep their words for it, and do not swap in your own name for the thing.

**What it looks like afterwards.** Say which in `WHY`:

- **Convenience:** no recent occasion, and a workaround they are content with.
- **Recurring friction:** a named occasion, repeated, with effort or cost each time.
- **Buying condition:** a real past choice of tool turned on it.
- **One-off:** it matters to this business for reasons other people in the role would not share.

Strong language moves a request into none of these. An occasion, a cost or a past decision does.

**Sources.** Tracing a request back to the outcome, the occasion, the workaround and the consequence is the book's method. The four labels are an operator adaptation, cut down for a simulation in which nothing is promised or built.
